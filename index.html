Professional Introduction

Professional Self-Assessment

A reflection on my growth throughout the Computer Science program and the technical and professional competencies demonstrated through my ePortfolio.

Completing the Bachelor of Science in Computer Science program has allowed me to develop from a student learning individual programming concepts into a more well-rounded computer science professional who can analyze problems, design solutions, evaluate existing systems, and improve software with the needs of users and stakeholders in mind. Throughout the program, I gained experience in software engineering, database development, algorithms and data structures, security, full-stack development, mobile application development, embedded systems, and artificial intelligence. Developing my ePortfolio during the capstone brought these areas together and gave me an opportunity to demonstrate not only what I have learned, but also how I can apply that knowledge to improve an existing software system.

My coursework has helped me recognize that one of my strongest abilities is approaching software as a complete system rather than focusing only on whether individual pieces of code function correctly. Earlier projects often concentrated on satisfying a particular technical requirement. As I progressed through the program, I learned to consider maintainability, usability, scalability, performance, and the needs of the people who will ultimately use the software. The capstone reinforced this perspective because I was required to review an existing artifact, identify opportunities for improvement, justify my design decisions, and implement enhancements that demonstrated multiple areas of computer science competency. This process strengthened my professional goal of pursuing software engineering and related technology roles where I can combine programming, problem solving, system design, and continuous improvement.

Collaboration and Communication

Collaboration and communication have also been important parts of my development. Computer science projects rarely exist independently of users, clients, or other members of a development organization. Throughout the program, I learned to consider requirements from multiple perspectives and translate those requirements into technical solutions. For example, while developing the Travlr Getaways full-stack web application, I worked from defined business requirements to create both customer-facing and administrative functionality. The project required me to consider how different users would interact with the system while coordinating front-end components, server-side functionality, RESTful API endpoints, authentication, and database operations. Experience such as this helped me understand that effective collaboration requires more than sharing technical knowledge; it also requires communicating design decisions clearly, documenting work, considering feedback, and ensuring that the final software supports stakeholder objectives.

Communicating technical information to stakeholders has become another area of growth. Different audiences require different levels of technical detail, and a developer must be able to explain both what a system does and why particular decisions were made. Coursework involving software design documents, project narratives, technical reports, and code reviews strengthened my ability to communicate complex information in an organized and understandable manner. My capstone code review was especially valuable because it required me to evaluate an existing application and explain its strengths, weaknesses, and planned improvements across software engineering and design, algorithms and data structures, and databases. Rather than simply changing code, I had to communicate the reasoning behind those changes. This is a skill I expect to use regularly when working with developers, managers, clients, and other stakeholders.

Algorithms and Data Structures

My understanding of algorithms and data structures has expanded significantly throughout the program. I learned that selecting an appropriate algorithm or data structure can directly affect the efficiency, scalability, and maintainability of a software system. Coursework in algorithms and artificial intelligence gave me experience evaluating different approaches to problem solving rather than relying exclusively on sequential processing or repeated searches. For example, my artificial intelligence coursework involved reinforcement learning using environments such as CartPole and TreasureMaze as well as neural-network applications involving image classification. These projects strengthened my understanding of how algorithms process data, evaluate alternatives, and improve results. During my capstone, I extended this knowledge by applying structures such as dictionaries, sets, sorted collections, binary search techniques, and heap-based candidate selection to an existing application. This progression demonstrates my ability to move from understanding algorithms academically to applying them within practical software solutions.

Software Engineering

Software engineering has been one of the most significant areas of growth during my degree program. Projects across multiple courses required me to move through different stages of the software development lifecycle, including requirements analysis, system design, implementation, testing, debugging, documentation, and enhancement. In my mobile application development coursework, for example, I created the EventFlow Android application using Java and SQLite. The application incorporated authentication, event creation and management, dashboard statistics, category filtering, analytics, settings, and SMS-related functionality. Developing these interconnected features reinforced the importance of modular design, interface consistency, validation, and careful management of application state.

Database Development

My database coursework complemented these software engineering skills by teaching me how applications persist, retrieve, and manage information effectively. I have worked with both relational and NoSQL database technologies, including SQLite and MongoDB. I learned to implement CRUD operations, design queries around application requirements, validate data, and connect database functionality to user-facing applications. These experiences showed me that database design should not be treated as an isolated backend concern. Query structure, indexing, data validation, access patterns, and database abstraction can all influence the reliability and performance of an application. My capstone provided an opportunity to apply these principles at a more advanced level by improving how an existing application interacts with its database.

Security Mindset

Security has become another important professional value throughout the program. My coursework demonstrated that security should be integrated into software development rather than added only after an application is complete. Through projects involving authentication, authorization, secure data handling, and software reverse engineering, I gained a stronger understanding of software vulnerabilities and defensive programming practices. In my reverse-engineering coursework, I analyzed compiled software using tools such as objdump, nm, and strings, examined assembly code, reconstructed program behavior, and evaluated software for potential vulnerabilities. That experience gave me a perspective on software that differs from traditional application development because it required me to consider how compiled programs can be examined and how implementation decisions may expose unintended information or weaknesses. Other projects reinforced practices such as protecting credentials, validating input, limiting unnecessary access, and using secure authentication mechanisms such as JSON Web Tokens. Together, these experiences have shaped my belief that security is a responsibility throughout the development lifecycle.

Adaptability and Professional Growth

The breadth of the Computer Science program has also strengthened my adaptability. In addition to traditional software applications, I worked with embedded systems using a Raspberry Pi, GPIO components, environmental sensors, LCD displays, and state-based application logic. I also explored artificial intelligence, machine learning, mobile development, full-stack web applications, database systems, and software security. Working across these different technologies required me to learn new tools, troubleshoot unfamiliar environments, read technical documentation, and transfer concepts from one area of computer science to another. This adaptability is especially valuable in a field where technologies continue to evolve, and professionals must continually expand their knowledge.

Creating my ePortfolio helped bring these individual experiences together into a clearer professional identity. Instead of viewing each course as a separate collection of assignments, I can now recognize recurring principles across my work: designing around user needs, selecting appropriate technical approaches, protecting and managing data, evaluating performance, maintaining code quality, and improving software through iteration. The capstone also showed me how much an existing system can be improved when it is evaluated from multiple perspectives rather than simply rewritten. These lessons have prepared me to enter the computer science field with both technical knowledge and an understanding that professional software development requires communication, continuous learning, thoughtful design, and accountability for the quality of the systems being produced.

Portfolio Artifacts

The technical artifacts that follow this self-assessment demonstrate how these competencies can work together within a complete software system. For my capstone, I selected the Grazioso Salvare rescue dashboard originally developed during my client/server development coursework. The original application connected Python, MongoDB, and the Dash framework to allow users to filter animal shelter records and display information through an interactive table, visualization, and map. I selected this artifact because its combination of user-interface design, application logic, data processing, and database integration provided opportunities to demonstrate several areas of computer science within one cohesive project.

Rather than treating the three enhancement categories as unrelated projects, I developed them as interconnected improvements to the same application. The first enhancement focuses on software engineering and design and transforms the original dashboard toward a more maintainable Rescue Match Recommendation System. The enhanced design separates responsibilities that were previously contained within a more tightly coupled implementation, creating clear boundaries between database access, application logic, recommendation functionality, and the user interface. This demonstrates my ability to evaluate software architecture and improve organization, readability, maintainability, and usability.

The second enhancement focuses on algorithms and data structures. Instead of relying primarily on repeated filtering operations, the enhanced application incorporates more purposeful approaches to organizing and evaluating animal records. Data structures and algorithms such as dictionaries, sets, sorted data, binary search techniques, and heap-based top-candidate selection support the recommendation process. These improvements demonstrate my ability to consider algorithmic efficiency when designing application functionality and to select structures based on how data will be accessed and processed.

The third enhancement focuses on databases and strengthens the relationship between the application and MongoDB. Improvements to database access emphasize separation of concerns, query efficiency, validation, configuration management, and indexing for commonly accessed information. These changes demonstrate that database design involves more than storing records; it requires considering security, performance, maintainability, and the ways application components interact with persistent data.

Together, the three enhancements demonstrate the progression of a single application from a functional academic project into a more thoughtfully designed software system. Software engineering principles provide the structure of the application, algorithms and data structures provide efficient methods for processing and ranking information, and database improvements provide reliable and efficient access to the data supporting those operations. Each enhancement is valuable independently, but their integration better represents how these competencies are used together in professional software development.

My ePortfolio therefore represents more than a collection of completed assignments. It demonstrates my ability to examine existing software critically, identify meaningful areas for improvement, implement technically appropriate solutions, and communicate the reasoning behind my decisions. Combined with the broader experiences I gained in mobile development, full-stack development, artificial intelligence, embedded systems, databases, security, and software reverse engineering, these artifacts reflect the range of skills I have developed throughout the Computer Science program. Most importantly, they demonstrate my readiness to continue developing as a computer science professional and to contribute to software solutions that are maintainable, secure, efficient, and responsive to the needs of users and organizations.
